Heston–COS & Deep Learning (EN)
Research report · 8th Olympiad in Econometrics & Applications, 2023

Calibration and Option Pricing of the Heston Model: An Empirical Analysis with Deep Learning

Tôn Nữ Triệu Mẫn · Ngô Thiện Mỹ · Hồ Ngọc Phương Trinh · Nguyễn Gia An — International University, VNU-HCM · Advisor: Assoc. Prof. Trần Vũ Khanh

Summary. The team calibrates the Heston model, priced with the COS method, on the full SPX option chain for 2022. Heston–COS fits market prices about 10 times better than Black–Scholes (RMSE 0.43% vs 4.05%). A neural network then learns the map from parameters to prices and is run in reverse to calibrate. Its error is small (R² = 0.991), which suggests a fast network can replace a slow numerical solver.

1Research questions

Black–Scholes assumes volatility is constant. Real markets disagree: option prices show a volatility smile and skew. The paper asks two questions.

  • On real data, is Heston good enough to serve as the benchmark model instead of Black–Scholes? Most earlier work tested on simulated data at a single point in time.
  • Can an artificial neural network (ANN) learn the Heston pricing function from parameters calibrated on real data, and be used for fast calibration?

2Overall pipeline

3Methodology

3.1 Black–Scholes (baseline)

The stock price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility σ:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \]

Building a riskless hedged portfolio and ruling out arbitrage gives the closed form for a European call:

\[ C^{BS} = S\,\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2),\qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} \]

3.2 The Heston model

Heston lets the variance \(v_t\) move on its own, following a mean-reverting CIR process. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), with \(X_t=\ln S_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dX_t &= \big(r - \tfrac12 v_t\big)dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW^X_t \\ dv_t &= \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \gamma\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^v_t \\ dW^X_t\,dW^v_t &= \rho\,dt \end{aligned} \]
ParameterMeaning
\(v_0\)Current (initial) variance
\(\theta\)Long-run variance that \(v_t\) reverts to
\(\kappa\)Speed of reversion to \(\theta\)
\(\gamma\)Volatility of volatility; controls the curvature of the smile
\(\rho\)Price–volatility correlation; \(\rho<0\) produces the left skew seen in equity markets

Its key strength is that it is an affine model, so the characteristic function of \(\ln S_T\) is known in closed form:

\[ \varphi(u) = \exp\!\Big( iur\tau + \tfrac{v_0}{\gamma^2}\tfrac{1-e^{-D\tau}}{1-Ge^{-D\tau}}(\kappa - i\rho\gamma u - D) \Big)\, \exp\!\Big( \tfrac{\kappa\theta}{\gamma^2}\big[\tau(\kappa - i\rho\gamma u - D) - 2\ln\tfrac{1-Ge^{-D\tau}}{1-G}\big] \Big) \]
\[ D = \sqrt{(\kappa - i\rho\gamma u)^2 + (u^2+iu)\gamma^2},\qquad G = \frac{\kappa - i\rho\gamma u - D}{\kappa - i\rho\gamma u + D} \]

The Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \ge \gamma^2\) keeps \(v_t>0\). The team drops this constraint during calibration because the unconstrained version fits the data better, in line with Ribeiro & Poulsen (2013).

3.3 The COS method (Fang & Oosterlee)

An option price is the discounted expected payoff. The density \(f(y\mid x)\) is unknown, but the characteristic function is known. COS truncates the integration range to \([a,b]\), expands the density as a cosine series, and reads each coefficient directly from \(\varphi\):

\[ V(x,t_0) \approx K e^{-r\tau}\,\mathrm{Re}\Big\{ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{}' \,\varphi\!\Big(\tfrac{k\pi}{b-a}\Big)\,U_k\,e^{ik\pi\frac{x-a}{b-a}} \Big\},\qquad x = \ln\tfrac{S_0}{K} \]
\[ U_k^{\text{call}} = \tfrac{2}{b-a}\big(\chi_k(0,b) - \psi_k(0,b)\big),\qquad [a,b] = \big[\zeta_1 - L\sqrt{\zeta_2},\ \zeta_1 + L\sqrt{\zeta_2}\big],\ L=10 \]

Here \(\zeta_1,\zeta_2\) are the first and second cumulants of \(\ln(S_T/K)\). The practical advantage: one evaluation prices a whole vector of strikes, which suits calibration well.

3.4 Calibrating Heston

Calibration is an inverse problem: find \(\Theta=(\rho,\gamma,\kappa,\theta,v_0)\) so that model prices are as close as possible to market mid prices:

\[ \Theta^* = \arg\min_{\Theta\in[l,u]} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i\big(V^{*}_i - V^{\Theta}_i\big)^2,\qquad \omega_i = \tfrac1N \]

This bound-constrained nonlinear problem is solved with L-BFGS-B, which approximates the inverse Hessian from a few recent pairs \((s_k,y_k)\) instead of storing the full matrix:

\[ H_{k+1} = (I-\rho_k s_k y_k^\top)H_k(I-\rho_k y_k s_k^\top) + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top,\quad \rho_k = \tfrac{1}{y_k^\top s_k} \]

3.5 Neural network: forward and backward pass

Forward pass. An MLP \(F(\cdot\mid w)\) learns the map from parameters to Heston–COS prices:

\[ F\big(\rho,\kappa,\theta,\gamma,v_0,\ \ln\tfrac{S_0}{K},\ \tau \,\big|\, w\big) \approx V^{\text{COS}},\qquad w^* = \arg\min_w \tfrac1n\sum \big(F - V^{\text{COS}}\big)^2 \]

Setup: 4 hidden layers × 100 neurons, ReLU, Glorot uniform initialisation, Nadam optimiser (Adam with Nesterov momentum), batch size 1024, 100 epochs.

Backward pass. Freeze the weights \(w^*\). The Heston parameters in the input layer now become the variables to learn, optimised with L-BFGS-B:

\[ \Theta^*_{\text{ANN}} = \arg\min_{\Theta} \tfrac1N\sum_{i}\big(F(\Theta, x_i, \tau_i \mid w^*) - P^*_i\big)^2 \]

The network is differentiable, so gradients with respect to \(\Theta\) come for free from backpropagation. That is why calibrating through the network is faster than calling COS repeatedly.

4Data

European call options on the S&P 500 index (SPX), end-of-day data from January 3 to December 30, 2022. Data are grouped by (quote date, expiry date); each group is one strike vector that COS prices in a single pass. The risk-free rate is fixed at 3.5%. Errors are measured on the normalised price \(V/K\) so different strikes are comparable.

2022 was a year of steep declines and high volatility for SPX, which makes it a hard test for a constant-volatility model.

5Results

5.1 Heston–COS vs Black–Scholes

The error distributions (Figures 5–6 in the original paper) show that most Heston–COS errors sit near zero, with a few outliers. BS errors are spread much wider, with a median around 2%. Heston’s MAE is far below its RMSE, meaning the error comes from a few large misses rather than being spread evenly.

6What the results mean

Stochastic volatility matters

The 10× gap between Heston and BS shows that the smile and skew are real features of SPX prices. A single σ cannot describe the whole price surface.

COS is fast enough for a full year

Pricing a whole strike vector in one pass makes daily recalibration across all of 2022 feasible.

An ANN can replace the solver

R² of 0.99 shows the network has learned the pricing function. The expensive part moves to offline training; at use time it is one pass through the network.

Calibration becomes differentiable

Parameter gradients come straight from the network, so optimisation is faster and more stable than finite differences on COS.

Read these results with care. Heston is calibrated separately for each (date, expiry) pair and errors are measured on the same data used for fitting, so they are in-sample. BS has one free parameter while Heston has five, so part of the gap comes from flexibility. The ANN is evaluated against Heston–COS prices, not directly against market prices, and the unit of the 5.5% forward-pass figure is not stated clearly.

7Practical applications

  • Real-time pricing and calibration on an options desk: the network replaces COS or Monte Carlo when thousands of contracts must be repriced every time the market moves.
  • Greeks and risk management: derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs (\(\partial V/\partial S\), \(\partial V/\partial v_0\)) come from autodiff, which helps with delta–vega hedging.
  • Building a consistent implied volatility surface with no arbitrage across strikes, for pricing illiquid or OTC products.
  • Vietnamese market: the framework applies to covered warrants (CW) and to VN30 options once they launch, where data are sparse and a structured model is needed.

8Suggested extensions

  • Out-of-sample testing: use day-t parameters to price day t+1, and track how stable Θ is over time.
  • Liquidity weighting: \(\omega_i \propto 1/\text{spread}_i\) or BS vega weights, so options with wide bid–ask spreads count less. Or measure error in implied volatility instead of price.
  • Global optimisation: L-BFGS-B only finds local minima. Differential Evolution can provide a starting point, then L-BFGS-B refines it.
  • Learn the inverse map directly: a network takes the whole price surface and returns Θ in one pass, with no optimisation loop.
  • Richer models: Bates (adds jumps), rough Heston or rough Bergomi for short maturities, and a term structure of rates instead of a fixed 3.5%.
  • Fairer comparisons: add baselines such as SVI or BS with an interpolated smile, and report run times for COS vs the ANN.
